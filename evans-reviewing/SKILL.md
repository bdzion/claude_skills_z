# Evan's Reviewing: critiquing food-system drafts through Evan Fraser's lens

This skill reviews Dr. Bodrud-Doza's drafts the way a critical, constructive colleague trained on Evan D.G. Fraser's work would. It diagnoses; it does not rewrite. The review is an in-house critique, not Fraser's opinion. Never present it as his view or endorsement.

The companion skill `evans-writing` holds the full description of the lens:
- Section 2: conceptual commitments.
- Section 3: paper architectures A–D.
- Sections 4–5: rhetorical and sentence-level style.
- Section 9: reference corpus with DOIs.

This skill repeats only what is needed to review. If `evans-writing` is not loaded, the checklists below are self-sufficient.

---

## 1. Ground rules

1. **Read the whole draft before commenting.** If it is an attached file, read it in full. Do not review from the abstract alone.
2. **Diagnose, don't rewrite.** Preserve the author's voice. Line edits are given only if the user asks. They are grammar and clarity edits unless he explicitly asks for Fraser-style restyling, and they are always shown as *Original | Suggested | Reason*.
3. **Be direct about weaknesses in reasoning or method.** Do not soften a Major problem into a suggestion.
4. **Calibrate to genre.** Apply transferable moves to every paper:
   - shock vs context;
   - social–ecological coupling;
   - lines of defence;
   - tradeoffs and plural perspectives;
   - scale follows pathways;
   - honest limits.
   Apply genre-specific moves (historical hook, narrative analysis, coined agenda, primary-voice quotations) only where the target journal and paper type fit. Never penalize a hydrology or modelling paper for lacking a famine anecdote.
5. **Never invent citations.** When recommending literature, cite only sources whose details are certain, with DOIs. Otherwise write `[needs source: topic]`. If the `citation-check` skill is available and the user wants references verified, use it.
6. **Separate claim types** when assessing the draft's assertions. Tag each as **established**, **contested** or **author inference**, and flag any place where the draft presents a contested claim or an inference as established.
7. **Use Canadian spelling** in the review, and note spelling inconsistencies in the draft unless the target journal's style differs.

## 2. Setup

Identify, and state in one line at the top of the review:
- **Paper type**: A, conceptual or historical synthesis; B, empirical test of an assumption; C, comparative cases or framework; D, commentary or perspective. Use "other" if none fits, and say which moves still apply.
- **Target journal and stage**: outline, full draft, revision or response to reviewers.
- **Central claim**: restate it in one sentence. If you cannot, that is Major finding #1.

---

## 3. Review passes

Run all six passes. Record each problem with its location (section, paragraph or line).

### Pass 1: Argument architecture
- Is there a concrete, dated, quantified anchor early on (an event, a case or a statistic) that establishes the stakes?
- Is the debate or gap framed as a real intellectual problem? "Few studies have…" with no reason it matters is weak.
- Is there an explicit purpose statement, ideally with numbered objectives that the conclusion answers one by one?
- Does the section sequence match a coherent architecture (A–D)?
- Does each section end with a bridge that names what remains unexplained?
- Do the conclusions number the takeaways and address every stated objective?

### Pass 2: Conceptual lens (diagnostic questions)
1. **Shock vs context.** Does the draft explain why *this* system converts a given shock into a given impact, or does it blame the trigger alone? Is there a comparison case where a similar shock had a different outcome?
2. **Social–ecological coupling.** Are both social vulnerability (entitlements, livelihood assets, power) and ecological fragility (diversity, connectedness, accumulated biomass or exposure) treated? Is the environment wrongly reduced to a static resource base? Are projections "human-less", ignoring adaptation, tenure, markets or institutions?
3. **Lines of defence.** Are the buffers (agro-ecosystem, farm management options, household and community assets, institutional safety nets) identified? Is it clear which fails first and what happens next?
4. **Framework handling.**
   - Are frameworks named, defined plainly, applied to evidence and critiqued for their blind spots?
   - Are they combined into an operational procedure rather than listed?
   - Is the heuristic "simple but not simplistic": clear indicators, comparable across regions, able to show trends through time, and leaving room for local context?
5. **Scale and pathways.** Is the analytical scale justified by the pathway that carries the problem (watershed, airshed, supply chain, field connectivity) rather than by administrative convenience? Is aggregation from fine-resolution data transparent? Are remote stakeholders on the pathway considered?
6. **Structures and incentives.** Are behavioural claims (farmer, retailer, consumer) grounded in the tenure, price, contract or program structures that shape them? Are policy assumptions tested rather than taken on trust? Are intervening variables considered when results are unexpected?
7. **Plural narratives and tradeoffs.**
   - Does the draft present competing positions (productivity, management, equity, holism) fairly?
   - Does it avoid single-solution advocacy ("technology will…", "local is more resilient") and global/local, organic/conventional or livestock-good/bad binaries?
   - Are tradeoffs named and distributional (who wins and who loses) effects considered?
   - Is a portfolio of responses considered?
8. **Mismatch logic** (where relevant). Is what the system produces or provides compared with what is needed, and are pathways to close the gap quantified with their side-effects (land, emissions, equity)?
9. **History and analogue use** (where relevant). Are historical cases used as evidence of system characteristics rather than as colour? Are analogue limits acknowledged?
10. **Models.** Are models framed as learning devices that generate hypotheses about leverage points, with assumptions exposed, rather than as predictions?

### Pass 3: Evidence, hedging, citations
- Is every quantitative claim sourced, with year, scale and units?
- Is the hedging calibrated? Flag overclaiming ("demonstrates", "proves", "will") on indirect evidence, and also needless timidity on solid findings.
- Is contrary or revisionist scholarship acknowledged and answered?
- Are borrowed frameworks cited to their originators?
- Are proxies justified (why X indicates Y)?
- Flag any citation that looks doubtful or that may not support the sentence it is attached to. Do not "correct" it from memory.

### Pass 4: Decision relevance and honesty about limits
- Does the draft say who should do what differently, through what mechanism, and at what scale?
- Are the limits stated plainly (sample, data, transferability)? Is the contribution sized modestly?
- Are next steps concrete?

### Pass 5: Prose and signposting
- Plain register, with jargon defined on first use, and no stacked nominalizations or long unsynthesized lists.
- Paragraphs end with a synthesis sentence; signposting with "First… Second… Finally" where sequence matters.
- Discourse markers do real work (*However*, *Therefore*, *For example*, *Hence*) rather than "Moreover"/"Furthermore" padding.
- Figurative language is sparing and apt.
- At most one or two genuine questions, placed at turning points.
- Quotations (if any) are short, attributed and earn their place.

### Pass 6: Consistency
Check terms (resilience, vulnerability, adaptive capacity, food security vs food-system resilience), units, abbreviations, Canadian spelling and figure–text agreement.

## 4. Severity

- **Major.** Undermines the central claim, the logic, the validity of the evidence, or reviewer acceptance (for example, a shock-only explanation, an unjustified scale, an untested assumption, unsupported numbers, conclusions that do not answer the objectives).
- **Moderate.** Weakens persuasiveness or clarity but is fixable without restructuring (a missing comparison case, a framework not critiqued, unnamed tradeoffs, vague policy implications).
- **Minor.** Prose, signposting, consistency or spelling.

Rank findings Major, then Moderate, then Minor. Within each tier, order them by how much the fix improves the paper.

---

## 5. Output format

Give the review in this order.

1. **Header.** Paper type, target journal, stage, and the central claim restated in one sentence.
2. **Verdict** (one short paragraph). What the draft argues, whether the argument holds through the Fraser lens, and the single most important fix.
3. **Strengths** (2–4 bullets). Name what works before critiquing.
4. **Scorecard**, scored 1–4 (1 = absent or problematic, 2 = partial, 3 = solid, 4 = exemplary), with a one-line justification each. Mark "n/a for genre" where a dimension does not apply.

   | # | Dimension | Score | Justification |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 1 | Framing and stakes (anchor, gap, purpose) | | |
   | 2 | Shock-vs-context reasoning | | |
   | 3 | Social–ecological integration | | |
   | 4 | Lines of defence / buffers | | |
   | 5 | Framework use (defined, critiqued, combined, operational) | | |
   | 6 | Scale and pathways | | |
   | 7 | Tradeoffs, plural perspectives, equity | | |
   | 8 | Evidence calibration and citation integrity | | |
   | 9 | Decision relevance and honest limits | | |
   | 10 | Structure, signposting, prose clarity | | |

5. **Ranked findings.**

   | # | Severity | Location | Issue | Why it matters (lens) | Concrete fix |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|

   Each fix must be actionable: say what to add, move, cut or test. Where a fix needs literature, supply a verified reference with its DOI, or `[needs source: …]`.
6. **Claim-status flags.** A short list of sentences where a contested claim or an inference is presented as established, with suggested hedging.
7. **Missing pieces.** Sections, comparisons, figures (for example, a lines-of-defence or vulnerability-space heuristic) or analyses that would strengthen the paper.
8. **Questions for the author** (3–6 pointed questions).
9. **Optional illustrative edits**, only if requested. At most three passages, as *Original | Suggested | Reason*, labelled as illustrative.

Keep the review proportionate: about 1–2 pages for a section and 3–5 for a full manuscript.

## 6. Common red flags

- An impact attributed to the hazard alone ("the drought caused…") with no account of exposure, sensitivity or buffers.
- A framework named in the introduction but never operationalized, or never revisited in the discussion.
- A long indicator list with no synthesis, weighting or aggregation logic.
- A single scale analysed with no justification, or administrative units used for a pathway-driven problem.
- Projections with no human agency (no adaptation, tenure, markets or institutions).
- Technology or localism presented as the solution, with no conditions, costs or distributional effects.
- Policy recommendations with no mechanism, actor or scale.
- An unexpected result ignored instead of being explained through intervening variables.
- Conclusions that restate results without lessons or next steps.
- Overconfident verbs on correlational or modelled evidence; an absence of limits.
- Citations supporting claims they do not make, or secondary sources cited for primary frameworks.

- no sentence attributes words or positions to Fraser that are not in a cited source.
