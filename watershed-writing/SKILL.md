# Watershed Writing: drafting watershed modelling and management scholarship

Use this skill to draft Dr. Bodrud-Doza's manuscripts on agricultural watershed modelling, BMP and wetland evaluation, nutrient transport, and watershed decision support. It is built from 13 papers that represent three recognizable writing traditions:

- **The Yang lineage (Guelph):** model-application and decision-support writing (Yang et al. 2010, 2016, 2025; Liu et al. 2016; Hanke et al. 2024 with Macrae and Yang).
- **The Basu group (Waterloo):** data-driven, first-person, mechanism-and-attribution writing (Van Meter & Basu 2017; Singh & Basu 2022; Ury et al. 2023; Shen et al. 2025; Mogollón et al. 2026).
- **The vulnerable-waters and wetlandscape consortium:** conceptual frameworks, principles and policy options (Creed et al. 2017; Lane et al. 2023; Thorslund et al. 2017).

The text produced is for Dr. Bodrud-Doza's own authorship. Paraphrase; never copy sentences from the corpus. Never attribute claims to these authors beyond what their papers show.

Companion skill: `watershed-reviewing` applies the same lens to critique a draft. Run its checklist on any full draft before handing it over.

---

## 1. Modes and setup

- **Outline mode.** Produce a section-by-section skeleton (Section 3), with each paragraph's purpose in one line, the evidence or figure each needs, and `[CITE: …]` placeholders.
- **Draft mode.** Write new prose in the conventions below.
- **Revise mode.** Use only when the user explicitly asks for his text to be restyled. Show every change as *Original | Revised | Reason*. Otherwise follow his standing preference: grammar and clarity only, preserve his voice, show changes. For his own sentence-level voice, combine this skill with `bodrud-doza-scholarly-voice`.

Before drafting, establish:
1. The paper type (A–F in Section 3).
2. The target journal (for example, *JGLR*, *J. Environ. Manage.*, *JAWRA*, *ERL*, *Nature*-family) and its structure: Methods in the middle or at the end; "Here we" voice or "This study" voice.
3. The central claim, in one sentence.
4. The watershed: name, area in km², dominant land use, drainage (for example, a tile-drained share), receiving water body, and policy target.
5. The model or data: model and version, period, stations, and scenarios.
6. Which results are in hand and which are pending.

If the user is present and one of these is missing, ask one short question. Otherwise state the assumption at the top of the draft.

## 2. Conceptual commitments (the watershed lens)

These ideas recur across the corpus. Use them to organize the argument and to interpret results, not as a list of keywords.

1. **The watershed is a connected system; connectivity governs function.** Water and materials move through surface runoff, tile drains, shallow groundwater and channels. Explain which pathway carries the constituent and when. Hydrologic connectivity and disconnection both matter: isolated or perched conditions often favour retention and biogeochemical processing.
2. **Effects do not scale linearly from site to watershed.** Aggregate effects of many wetlands (a "wetlandscape") differ from single-wetland functions. Combined BMP effects at the outlet are smaller than the sum of individual BMPs, because upland practices change the load reaching lowland practices. Cumulative loss of small features matters even when one loss is undetectable.
3. **Nonlinearity, thresholds and asymmetry.**
   - Degradation from wetland loss accelerates as loss increases, while restoration benefits decelerate (Yang et al. 2016).
   - Watersheds can cross thresholds into alternative states (Lane et al. 2023).
   - Input–output relationships show hysteresis (Van Meter & Basu 2017).
   - Small features can be disproportionately effective per unit area (Shen et al. 2025).
   State the shape of the response, not just its magnitude.
4. **Legacies and time lags.** Decades of surplus N and P are stored in soils, groundwater and sediments. Water quality can lag management by decades, and wetlands with legacy soil P can switch from sink to source. Frame targets and expectations accordingly, and give realistic recovery timescales.
5. **Seasonality and climate change.**
   - In cold agricultural regions, most export occurs in the non-growing season during a few events.
   - Warming winters shift flow toward tile drainage and raise dissolved nutrient export.
   - Seasonal analysis reveals signals that annual totals hide.
6. **Attribution: climate versus human drivers.** Separate natural from managed influences (dams, canals, impervious cover, tile drainage). Human activity can amplify or dampen climate trends. Name the drivers and test them, for example with nested subwatersheds, natural vs managed contrasts or multivariate models.
7. **Variability and context dependence.**
   - The same practice can be a sink or a source depending on hydraulic loading, influent concentration, legacy and design.
   - "One size does not fit all."
   - The evidence base itself can be biased (for example, mesocosms outperform field wetlands).
   Report ranges and controls, not just medians.
8. **Spatial targeting and cost-effectiveness.** Express effects per unit area as well as in total. Priority areas are where the process-based effect per unit is largest, not necessarily where the most loss occurred. Link biophysical effects to costs and to optimization under multiple ecosystem-service targets.
9. **Translate results into decision terms.** Anchor numbers to:
   - the governing target (for example, the GLWQA reductions for Lake Erie, or a lake-specific P target);
   - equivalent infrastructure (wastewater treatment capacity, treatment cost);
   - ecosystem-service value;
   - a share of basin load.
   This is the corpus's most consistent move from result to relevance.
10. **Protection and restoration as complementary policy levers.** Halting loss is often cheaper and more effective than restoring. Policy options form a gradient from protecting all features, to protecting those with demonstrable effects, to protecting a functional portfolio. Each option has data, resource and opportunity-cost trade-offs, and "do nothing" is not neutral.
11. **Data, mapping and uncertainty are part of the finding.** Unmapped features cannot be managed. Model reliability depends on input data, process representation (for example, saturation-excess versus infiltration-excess runoff in Southern Ontario), parameterization and calibration. Every modelling paper ends with specific monitoring and model-development needs, such as edge-of-field monitoring or on-site wetland data.
12. **Institutions, markets and stakeholders.** Conservation costs fall on-site while benefits accrue off-site, so markets are missing and conservation is under-supplied (Yang et al. 2010). Decision-support tools must be co-developed with stakeholders and judged on usability, not just function (Yang et al. 2025).
13. **Food–water bidirectionality.** Food production degrades water, and changing water conditions constrain food production (Mogollón et al. 2026). Use this as the bridge when watershed work connects to food-system resilience.

## 3. Paper architectures

**A. Watershed model application or scenario study** (Liu et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2016; Hanke et al. 2024)
1. *Policy and problem context:* eutrophication, harmful algal blooms, the governing initiative or target, and the role of the watershed.
2. *Process background:* the pathways and mechanisms relevant to the constituent.
3. *Prior modelling, organized by direction:* "One direction… Another direction…", ending with what those studies did not do ("However, these studies did not…").
4. *Purpose:* "The purpose of this study is to…", with numbered objectives where helpful.
5. *Study area:* area in km², land use, soils, drainage, climate normals, receiving water and why the watershed suits the question.
6. *Model setup:* model and version, data sources and resolution, management data (crops, fertilizer, tillage, tile drains, reservoirs, wetlands, point sources), and any modules or adaptations to Canadian conditions.
7. *Calibration and validation:*
   - warm-up, calibration and validation periods;
   - gauging and water-quality stations;
   - the parameters calibrated and the tool used;
   - performance measures (NSE, PBIAS, R², and KGE if used) judged against stated criteria;
   - graphical comparisons;
   - performance by season and by flow condition.
8. *Scenarios:*
   - a baseline;
   - gradients of loss or restoration, or individual and combined BMPs, or climate ensembles (GCMs and RCPs or SSPs) with clearly stated future periods;
   - the representation of each BMP or feature;
   - the extent and placement of each scenario.
9. *Results:*
   - relative change from baseline (%) and absolute change (t/y);
   - per-hectare effects;
   - spatial variation by subarea;
   - seasonal shifts;
   - concentration and load reported separately.
10. *Discussion:*
    - the mechanism behind each key result (pathway shift, interaction among practices);
    - the result in decision terms (targets, infrastructure equivalence);
    - consistency with independent evidence;
    - specific uncertainties.
11. *Conclusions:* what was done, the key quantitative findings, limitations, and the data and model-development needs.

**B. Data-driven or empirical analysis** (Van Meter & Basu 2017; Singh & Basu 2022; Shen et al. 2025)
1. Open with the global-to-regional problem, illustrated by named systems.
2. State a *paradox or mismatch*: inputs fall but concentrations rise; annual trends show no human signal but seasonal trends do.
3. Explain why existing studies cannot resolve it (annual focus, natural watersheds only, no link between input trajectories and outputs).
4. "Here, we…" with the scale of evidence (n watersheds or subwatersheds, years, area).
5. Give results with headline numbers and the controls identified (tile drainage, slope, dams, size class).
6. Set out implications for targets and management.
7. Place Methods at the end if the journal requires it.

**C. Meta-analysis** (Ury et al. 2023)
1. A popular management strategy whose performance is variable.
2. The dataset: n sites, site-years, timescales.
3. Central tendency *and* range, split by form (TP versus PO₄³⁻).
4. Controls on source or sink behaviour.
5. Bias in the evidence base.
6. Temporal variability.
7. Recommendations: enhance retention and avoid release.
8. Data availability, with a DOI for the dataset.

**D. Conceptual framework or synthesis review** (Lane et al. 2023; Thorslund et al. 2017; Mogollón et al. 2026)
1. Definitions up front, using a box where the journal allows.
2. The significance and extent of the system.
3. The conceptual framework, with a figure.
4. How disturbances act on it.
5. Numbered principles for management.
6. Scientific needs.
7. A conclusion that restates the principles.
Highlights or key points are short declarative claims.

**E. Perspective or policy options** (Creed et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2010)
1. The governance gap and a timely trigger.
2. The scientific rationale, using values and extents in a box.
3. Tiered options, ordered from most to least protective, each with its scientific basis, its resource needs and its trade-offs.
4. The consequence of doing nothing.
5. A call to action. Close by tying the action to "the best available scientific evidence".

**F. Decision-support tool or integrated modelling** (Yang et al. 2025)
1. The need: models are too complex for the stakeholders who must use them.
2. The integrated modelling engine (economic, hydrologic, soil carbon, optimization) and how the models exchange data across scales.
3. The tool's functions, numbered.
4. Case-study demonstrations.
5. The stakeholder engagement process, covering data, development and training.
6. The stakeholders' evaluation, *including criticism*.
7. Limitations and next products.

## 4. Paragraph-level moves

- **Problem, then process, then gap, then purpose.** Every introduction narrows from a societal problem to a process question to a specific gap ("However, these studies did not simulate…") and ends with a purpose sentence.
- **Organize prior work by direction or approach**, not author by author.
- **Pair contrasts:** loss vs restoration; natural vs managed; source vs sink; amplification vs dampening; small vs large water bodies; individual vs cumulative.
- **Quantify, then interpret, then anchor.** Give the number, then the mechanism, then the decision meaning. For example: *"A 28% loss of riparian wetland would add enough phosphorus to cancel the removal capacity of the local treatment plant."* (illustrative structure, not a corpus quote).
- **Frame mechanisms as hypotheses** when the model or data cannot isolate them ("We hypothesize that warmer winters…").
- **Check against independent evidence.** "These projections are consistent with observed trends in…"
- **Limit statements are specific.** Name the input data, the process representation, the parameters, and the monitoring that would reduce uncertainty.
- **Close with an implication for management**, not just a restatement of results.

## 5. Sentence-level conventions

- **Voice depends on the journal.**
  - Elsevier applied journals in the Yang tradition use "This study…" and the passive for methods ("The model was calibrated…").
  - ERL, *Nature*-family and review journals in the Basu tradition use active first person ("Here, we quantify…", "We find…").
  - Choose one per manuscript and keep it consistent.
- **Purpose statements.** "The purpose of this study is to…" in the Yang tradition; "Here, we…" in the Basu tradition.
- **Discourse markers that do work:** *However*, *In contrast*, *For example*, *Furthermore*, *In addition*, *Specifically*, *Overall*. Avoid stacking them.
- **Hedging.** Use *may*, *likely* and *suggest* for inference, and *show* and *found* for direct results. Reserve *demonstrate* for robust evidence.
- **Number reporting:**
  - SI units with explicit time bases (t/y or t yr⁻¹, kg/ha/y, g·m⁻²·yr⁻¹, mg L⁻¹);
  - one notation style per manuscript;
  - percentages with their baseline;
  - ranges for ensembles and meta-analyses;
  - area in km² and ha.
- **Constituent notation.** Define TP, TN, SS, NO₃⁻ and PO₄³⁻ (SRP/DRP) on first use. Keep concentration, load and yield distinct. State whether results are flow-weighted.
- **Define every class or threshold:** vulnerable waters; non-floodplain wetlands; size classes (for example, <10^4.5 m²); seasons by month; scenario names.
- **Present scenarios in a table:** name, description, extent, location, and the parameters changed.
- **Spelling.** Canadian (modelling, behaviour, centre), unless the target journal's style differs (for example, "modeling" in ASABE or JAWRA). Keep acronyms consistent ("BMP" for best management practice, or "BMP/beneficial management practice" in Canadian programs). Define them once.

## 6. Evidence and citation rules

- **Never invent a citation, value, DOI or policy target.** Write `[CITE: claim — needs source]` or `[VALUE: needs source]` instead.
- Give DOIs for supplied references and flag any uncertain details.
- In notes and drafts, tag key claims as **established**, **contested** or **inference**, and match the strength of the hedging to the tag.
- For model performance criteria, cite the standard the manuscript uses:
  - Moriasi et al. 2007, *Trans. ASABE* 50(3): 885–900, https://doi.org/10.13031/2013.23153;
  - Moriasi et al. 2015, *Trans. ASABE* 58(6): 1763–1785, https://doi.org/10.13031/trans.58.10715.
  State the thresholds the manuscript adopts rather than paraphrasing them from memory.
- Cite policy targets and loads from the governing agreement or agency report, with year.
- Make data and code availability explicit. Python first, with stated CRS, units and time steps, following the user's preference.

## 7. Applying the lens to Dr. Bodrud-Doza's work

These are applications for his manuscripts, not claims from the corpus.

- **Tile-drained Great Lakes BMP studies** (Medway Creek, Ausable Bayfield, Lake Huron and Lake Erie basins):
  - non-growing-season export and the tile pathway;
  - non-additivity of BMP combinations;
  - per-hectare effects and spatial targeting;
  - legacy P and expected lags;
  - results anchored to GLWQA or Healthy Lake Huron targets.
- **IMWEBs, SWAT and ACPF modelling:** report model adaptations, management inputs and seasonal and high-flow performance explicitly. State the field-to-watershed aggregation logic.
- **Wetland and small-water-body work:** size-class effects, connectivity, protection versus restoration, and source/sink risk.
- **Decision support and GeoAI:** co-development with stakeholders, usability evaluation, and honest reporting of stakeholder criticism.
- **Food–water linkage:** use bidirectional framing when the paper bridges to food-system resilience.

## 8. Drafting workflow

1. Confirm the mode and setup items (Section 1).
2. Write the central claim and the gap sentence first.
3. Choose the architecture (A–F) and outline it. List the figures and tables at the outline stage (study area map, calibration plots, scenario table, response curves, spatial maps, seasonal panels).
4. Draft the methods with full reproducibility detail.
5. Draft the results with numbers, baselines and units checked.
6. Draft the discussion following mechanism, then decision anchor, then consistency check, then uncertainty.
7. Insert `[CITE]` and `[VALUE]` placeholders. Never fill them from memory.
8. Self-check against `watershed-reviewing` and fix Major issues.
9. Deliver the draft with a note listing assumptions, open placeholders and claims tagged *contested* or *inference*.

## 10. Limits of this style model

- The corpus mixes several author groups. The skill describes shared conventions plus each group's distinct voice; it does not model one author.
- The corpus contains no IMWEBs, ACPF or tile-drainage field-monitoring papers. For those, follow the target journal and standard practice, and ask the user to add exemplars.
- Policy contexts are mostly Ontario, the Great Lakes and the US. Verify current targets and regulations before citing, because they change.
