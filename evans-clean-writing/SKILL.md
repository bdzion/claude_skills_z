# Evan's Clean Writing: plain-language food-system pieces in Evan Fraser's essay style

Use this skill when Dr. Bodrud-Doza needs food-system writing that a minister, a farmer, a journalist or a general reader can follow on one read. Typical pieces:
- op-eds;
- newsletter or Substack essays;
- blog posts;
- policy briefs and briefing notes;
- lay summaries for grants;
- explainers for The Conversation.

It is built from Evan Fraser's public essays in *Feeding a Fragile World* ("Buffers. Food. Crisis."), August–October 2026.

## 1. The ideas these essays carry

Plain language is not thin thinking. Every piece should still rest on one or more of these ideas.

1. **Shocks are inevitable; crises aren't.** Droughts, wars, price spikes and policy surprises will happen. Whether they become crises depends on the buffers in place: stocks, early warning, safety nets, trade rules, finance and local capacity.
2. **The danger is many buffers stressed at once.** Avoid both alarmism and complacency. The honest message is usually "not collapse, but several independent buffers under strain simultaneously".
3. **Earlier is cheaper.** Relief, diplomacy and investment work best before a crisis unfolds. Say so, and name what can be done *now*.
4. **Enough food is not the same as food reaching people.** Aggregate supply can be adequate while stocks sit in the wrong places and poorer buyers are outbid. Shortage is usually about access, price and distribution.
5. **"Unprecedented" usually has precedents.** Check a claim of novelty against history (the Dust Bowl, Smoot–Hawley, the grain embargoes, famines). The lesson is often the pattern.
6. **Food is a lever.** It can be a weapon (coercion, export bans, sieges) or a shield (reliable trade, aid, stability). The choice is political.
7. **Three overlapping scales.** Global, regional and local forces interact. Name which scale a problem or solution sits at.
8. **Technology helps only with governance and local partners.** Data and AI work best where data are richest, which is often where need is lowest. Without local ownership, new tools can add a new dependency.
9. **Policy decisions are hazards too.** The next crisis may start with a government announcement, not a drought.
10. **Name the trade-offs and the people who bear them.** Who gains, who pays, and who is left adrift.

## 2. Piece architecture

### A. The 1,000–1,800-word essay or op-ed (default)

1. **Title and deck.** The title is short and concrete, often a paradox or inversion: "Shocks are inevitable, crises aren't"; "X might sound like good news. It isn't."; "Food as a weapon. Food as a shield." The deck is one or two sentences stating the thesis outright.
2. **Opening scene (2–3 short paragraphs).** Use a real moment from the author's life: a meeting, a conversation, a field visit, a line in a report that caught his eye. Give the place, the people and one sensory or human detail. **Only use real experiences the user supplies. Ask for one rather than invent it.** Alternatives: a sweeping historical opener ("Throughout history…"), or a striking number.
3. **Pivot.** One or two sentences that turn from the scene to the issue and state why it matters now.
4. **Body: 3–5 sections with plain headings.** Headings are short phrases or questions ("A food dividend?", "What's a middle power to do?", "Waiting till it's too late"). Inside:
   - numbered takeaways ("Three things stood out. First… Second… Third…");
   - **or** named scenarios (two-word labels, from best to worst, each with its assumption and consequence), presented as "conversation starters, not predictions";
   - **or** contrasting futures (one bad, one better), each with a concrete person-scale vignette, such as a smallholder in the Sahel getting advice from an AI trained on Iowa cornfield data;
   - **or** historical precedents set out in order.
5. **The caveat.** Add one paragraph that keeps the argument honest: what the evidence does *not* say and where the uncertainty lies.
6. **What we can do.** Give concrete, named actions with an actor attached (governments, donors, industry, universities). For example: pre-agreed finance triggered at price thresholds; transparent reporting of grain stocks; early-warning systems that are owned locally; foresight councils outside election cycles.
7. **Close.** Return to the opening scene and end on a call to act, a pointed question, or an open invitation ("I welcome feedback on what's missing"). Humility is allowed: "this is far from settled in my own mind."
8. **Sources and method note.** List links to data sources. If a figure was computed, say how (deflator, base year, denominator). If AI tools were used for analysis, say so plainly.

### B. The 300–500-word data note
Use this shape:
1. The surprising line or number.
2. Why it looks like X but is really Y ("this is not good news").
3. One figure that "speaks for itself".
4. A short method footnote.
5. Sources.

### C. Policy brief or briefing note (1–2 pages)
1. A bottom line up front: 2–3 sentences.
2. Context: the shock and the buffer that is failing.
3. Three findings, numbered.
4. Options with their trade-offs.
5. Recommended actions with an owner and timing.
6. Sources.
The voice is calm and first-person plural. Keep the opening scene to one line, or drop it.

### D. Lay summary (100–250 words)
Write four parts:
1. The problem in one human-scale sentence.
2. What we will do.
3. Why it matters for people.
4. What changes if it works.
Use no acronyms.

## 3. Clean sentences

Fraser's essays average about 22 words a sentence, with roughly a quarter over 30 words. For "clean" writing, keep his rhythm but tighten it.

- **Length targets:**
  - average sentence 15–20 words;
  - no sentence over 30 words without a reason;
  - at least one sentence under 10 words in every section.
- **One idea per sentence.** Break chains of "and…, which…, while…".
- **Active voice, real verbs.** Write "Governments cut funding", not "A reduction in funding occurred". Turn nominalizations back into verbs.
- **Lead with the point.** Each paragraph's first sentence says what the paragraph proves. Its last sentence lands the consequence.
- **Make mechanisms explicit as a chain:**
  > Low water in the Panama Canal → less cargo per ship → higher costs → more friction in an already strained system.

  Write the chain out in plain sentences.
- **Define a technical term in the same breath:**
  > Sulphur, in the form of sulphuric acid, is essential for making phosphate fertilizer.
- **Use short punch lines at turns:** "It isn't." "Yet." "The next food crisis may not begin with a drought."
- **Paradox and contrast carry the thesis:** weapon/shield; inevitable/avoidable; abundant/out of reach.
- **Connectives.** Use "But", "For example", "In other words", "Of course", "Equally", "Here". Starting a sentence with "But" is fine. Avoid "Furthermore", "Moreover" and "It is important to note that".
- **Pronouns:**
  - "I" for the author's experience and opinions ("I believe", "In my view");
  - "we" for society and readers;
  - occasionally "let's".
  Label opinion as opinion.
- **Hedging.** Use one honest hedge per claim (could, may, likely, arguably). Don't stack them ("may potentially possibly").
- **Register.** Conversational but not flippant. A little idiom is fine ("stack the odds", "lean in", "bang on about"). Use at most one or two per piece, and no sarcasm.
- **Metaphor.** Pick one governing image per piece and stay with it: buffers, shield, lever, cascade, chokepoint, or the fabric of stability.

### Plain swaps

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| utilize, leverage (verb) | use |
| facilitate, enable | help, let |
| in order to | to |
| a significant number of | many (or the number) |
| at this point in time | now |
| stakeholders | name them: farmers, processors, ministries, food banks |
| resilience (undefined) | the ability to absorb a shock and keep people fed |
| food insecurity | not having reliable access to enough healthy food (on first use) |
| operationalize | put into practice |
| synergies, nexus, holistic, transformative | say what actually connects or changes |
| mitigate | reduce, soften |
| anthropogenic | human-caused |
| BMPs, IPC, SOFI, CFS (acronyms) | spell out or drop; keep at most two per piece |

## 4. Numbers

- Use one or two numbers per paragraph, rounded, each with an anchor that makes it mean something:
  - a ratio ("1 in 4 Canadians");
  - a change ("from 86 conflicts in 2010 to 184 in 2024");
  - a share of a peak ("55 per cent of the 2022 peak").
- Every number gets a source and a year, either in the text or in the sources list.
- Adjust for inflation and population when comparing money over time, and say you did.
- Say what the number means in the next sentence.
- Keep units consistent. Write out "per cent" (Canadian style), or follow the outlet's house style.
- Prefer one clear figure to a paragraph of statistics. A figure caption states the takeaway, not just the variables.

---

## 5. Honesty rules

- **Never invent** quotes, statistics, citations, meetings, colleagues or personal moments. If a scene or quote is needed, ask the user for it, or leave `[SCENE: user to supply]` / `[SOURCE: needs link]`.
- **Separate fact, estimate and opinion** in the wording itself: "Data show…", "Estimates suggest…", "I believe…". Follow the user's standing preference to keep established findings, contested ones and his own inference visibly distinct.
- **Scenarios are not forecasts.** Label them as conversation starters and state their assumptions.
- **Avoid both alarm and comfort.** If the headline is scary, include the caveat (for example, that aggregate food supply is adequate). If the news sounds good, check whether it is ("stabilized at 55 per cent of peak").
- **Credit people** by name and role when their ideas shape the piece, and only with their actual words or ideas.
- **Disclose AI assistance** for data work if it was used, as Fraser does in his method notes.

## 6. Applying this to Dr. Bodrud-Doza's work

These are illustrations written for this skill, not Fraser's text.

**Academic to clean, watershed:**
> *Academic:* "Implementation of targeted BMPs in tile-drained subwatersheds demonstrated significant reductions in dissolved reactive phosphorus loading under projected climate scenarios."
> *Clean:* "Tile drains carry fertilizer from fields straight into streams. When farmers in the Ausable Bayfield area put conservation practices on the fields that leak the most, our models show far less phosphorus reaching Lake Huron, even under a wetter future climate."

**Academic to clean, stress-testing:**
> *Academic:* "Anticipatory action frameworks facilitate pre-emptive resource mobilization contingent upon threshold exceedance."
> *Clean:* "Agree on the trigger before the crisis. If fertilizer prices pass a set level, the money moves automatically, before families start skipping meals."

**Academic to clean, Earth observation:**
> *Academic:* "Remote-sensing-derived indicators exhibit reduced predictive skill in data-sparse regions."
> *Clean:* "Satellites see everywhere, but the models that read them learn from places with good ground data. They work worst where help is needed most."

When numbers or place names are used in such rewrites, use only figures and results the user has actually produced.

## 7. Workflow

1. **Settle the basics:**
   - piece type (A–D);
   - outlet and word limit;
   - reader (who, and what they already know);
   - the one-sentence thesis;
   - the call to action.
   If the user is present and the thesis or a real opening scene is missing, ask one short question.
2. **Write the title and deck first.** If the deck can't state the thesis in two sentences, the thinking isn't done.
3. **Outline:** scene, pivot, three to five headed sections, caveat, actions, close.
4. **Draft fast in the voice above.** Mark `[SOURCE]`, `[SCENE]` and `[NUMBER]` gaps instead of filling them from memory.
5. **Run the clean pass (Section 8).**
6. **Deliver** the piece with a short note listing open placeholders and any claim tagged as estimate or opinion.

## 8. Clean pass checklist (run before delivery)

- [ ] Title and deck state the thesis; a reader who stops there still gets it.
- [ ] Opening scene is real, short and returns at the end.
- [ ] Every paragraph opens with its point.
- [ ] No sentence over 30 words without a reason; at least one short punch line per section.
- [ ] Jargon translated, acronyms at most two, each term defined on first use.
- [ ] Each number has an anchor, a source and a year.
- [ ] The caveat paragraph is present; opinions are labelled.
- [ ] Actions name an actor and a timeframe.
- [ ] One governing metaphor, not three.
- [ ] Throat-clearing cut ("It is worth noting", "In today's world", "This essay will").
- [ ] Read aloud: nothing trips the tongue.
- [ ] Proofread separately for typos, doubled words, apostrophes and missing words. Fast-published essays often carry small slips, so catch them.
- [ ] Spelling is Canadian (behaviour, centre, defence, program, fertilizer, sulphur), or the outlet's house style if it differs.

Optional self-check script (Python, run on a .txt or .md draft):

```python
# Plain-language metrics for a draft; flags long sentences.
# Assumes UTF-8 text; headings and lists count as sentences only if they end in . ! or ?
import re, sys, statistics as st
text = open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8").read()
sents = [s.strip() for s in re.split(r'(?<=[.!?])\s+', text) if len(s.split()) > 2]
lens = [len(s.split()) for s in sents]
words = re.findall(r"[A-Za-z']+", text)
print(f"sentences={len(sents)} mean={st.mean(lens):.1f} median={st.median(lens)}")
print(f"% over 30 words={100*sum(l>30 for l in lens)/len(lens):.0f}  % under 10={100*sum(l<10 for l in lens)/len(lens):.0f}")
print(f"long words (8+ letters)={100*sum(len(w)>=8 for w in words)/len(words):.1f}%  (Fraser essays ~18%, his papers ~23%)")
for s, l in zip(sents, lens):
    if l > 30: print(f"[{l}] {s[:120]}...")
```
