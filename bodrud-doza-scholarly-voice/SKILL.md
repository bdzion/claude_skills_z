# Dr. Bodrud-Doza's scholarly voice

Reference sample: Bodrud-Doza et al. (2023), *Science of the Total Environment* 905, 167118 (review of spatial decision support systems and tools for precision conservation). These traits come from analysing that paper.

## Two modes. Pick one before writing.

1. **Editing his text** (he supplies the prose): change grammar, punctuation, agreement, articles and obvious clarity errors only. Do not restructure, add synonyms, or "elevate" wording. Keep his terms, acronyms, sentence order and connectives. Return a list of the changes made.
2. **Drafting new text** (he asks Claude to write): follow the voice profile below. Mark every claim that needs a citation as [CITE: claim]. Never invent a reference, DOI, statistic or year.

## Voice profile

**Argument architecture**
- Introductions funnel from global to specific: global pressure (population growth, food demand, climate change) → environmental consequence (NPS pollution, eutrophication, hypoxia) → policy and investment response → the persistent problem ("Despite this investment, ... remains a persistent and complex challenge") → the gap ("... is underexamined") → the aim ("This review aims to address this gap through ...").
- State objectives as an enumerated sequence: "First, we ... Second, we ... Third, we propose ...", followed by an "overarching goal" sentence.
- Always tie findings back to the end user: decision-makers, watershed managers, farmers, conservation authorities.
- Conclusions restate the broad challenge, summarise the contribution (often a framework presented as a "roadmap"), name the main challenge ahead, and close on a forward-looking sentence about future research.

**Sentence level**
- Sentences average about 25–27 words. Mostly declarative compound or complex sentences, often with a participial tail ("..., resulting in ...").
- Connectives, in order of preference: However > Therefore > Furthermore > For example > Moreover / For instance > In addition. Rarely Thus, Hence, Notably, Consequently. Never "delve", "tapestry", "landscape of" (as metaphor), "crucial role", "in today's world".
- Contrast with "While X, Y" and "Despite X, Y".
- Hedging is measured. Use "can" for potential and "will require" or "needs to" for normative claims; "may" sparingly. No over-claiming adjectives (groundbreaking, novel, unprecedented).
- First-person plural only for structural moves ("we discuss", "we propose"). Otherwise use "this study" or "this review".
- Triadic lists are a signature: "field, farm, and watershed scales"; "socio-economic, environmental, and ecological outcomes"; "right practice, right place, right time" (7Rs).

**Evidence and terminology**
- Dense evidence: most substantive sentences carry author–year citations, often two (Name et al., Year; Name and Name, Year).
- Anchor context with concrete quantities and units (percentages, ha, dollars, years, program names).
- Define acronyms at first use and use them consistently afterwards: NPS, BMPs, SDSS/SDST, CSA, ACPF, SWAT, IMWEBs, IPIM, 7Rs.
- Name policies and programs precisely (Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement, 2012 amendment; Environmental Farm Plan; GLRI).
- Use "precision conservation", "location-specific", "critical source areas", "spatial trade-offs", "cost-effectiveness (e.g., highest environmental gain per dollar spent)".

**Spelling**
- Follow the target journal's convention (Elsevier STOTEN and ISWCR used "modeling"). For Canadian venues, grants and university documents, use Canadian spelling ("modelling", "programme" not used, "behaviour").

## Workflow
1. Confirm the mode (edit vs draft), target venue and section.
2. If drafting, outline the funnel or move structure first and get approval for long sections.
3. Write, insert [CITE: …] placeholders, and list them at the end.
4. Self-check against the profile: connectives, sentence length, hedging, triads, acronym definitions, no banned words.
5. Remind him that journals require a generative-AI use disclosure when drafted text is used.
