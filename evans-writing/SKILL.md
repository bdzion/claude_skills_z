# Evan's Writing: drafting food-system scholarship in Evan Fraser's style

This skill helps Dr. Bodrud-Doza draft his own food-system manuscripts using the argument structure, conceptual frameworks and prose habits found in Evan D.G. Fraser's published work. The text produced is for Dr. Bodrud-Doza's authorship. Do not attribute words to Fraser, invent Fraser quotations, or imply his endorsement. Paraphrase rather than copy his sentences.

Companion skill: `evans-reviewing` uses the same lens to critique a draft. Run its checklist on any full draft before handing it over.

---

## 1. Modes

Settle the mode before writing.

- **Outline mode.** Produce a section-by-section skeleton (Section 3), with the purpose of each paragraph in one line, the evidence each needs, and `[CITE: …]` placeholders.
- **Draft mode.** Write new prose in the Fraser style (Sections 2–6).
- **Revise-toward-Fraser mode.** Use only when the user explicitly asks for his existing text to be restyled. Show every change as a table with columns *Original | Revised | Reason*. Otherwise follow his standing preference: grammar and clarity only, preserve his voice, show changes. If he wants his own sentence-level voice with Fraser's argument architecture, combine this skill with `bodrud-doza-scholarly-voice`.

Before drafting, establish:
1. The paper type (A–D in Section 3).
2. The target journal and its genre conventions.
3. The single central claim.
4. The system and the shock or stressor at issue.
5. The evidence already in hand.
6. The audience: academic, policy or mixed.

If the user is present and one of these is missing, ask one short question. If he is not present, take the most reasonable reading and state it at the top of the draft.

## 2. Conceptual commitments (the "Fraser lens")

These are the ideas that recur across the corpus. Use them as organizing logic, not as decoration.

1. **The shock is rarely the explanation; the context is.** Similar triggers produce very different outcomes. A common pest (*Phytophthora*, which had recurred for decades) destroyed Irish society in 1845. Yet the 1991–92 southern African drought, with yields 56% below normal, produced no famine deaths in South Africa. Ask: why did *this* system turn a modest disturbance into a catastrophe? Pair cases where a similar shock had different impacts.
2. **Vulnerability is social *and* ecological, and the two co-produce each other.** On the social side, use Sen's entitlements: direct (grow), indirect (buy with income) and transfer (aid). Hunger is an entitlement failure, not just a supply failure. On the ecological side, use panarchy-style indicators: diversity/resilience, connectedness and accumulated "wealth" (biomass) that makes a system "an accident waiting to happen" (Holling's phrase). The same socio-economic forces that impoverish people can simplify the agro-ecosystem they depend on. Note Fraser's refinement: ecologically fragile systems tend to be simple, wealthy and connected, whereas socially vulnerable groups are often simple, poor and *isolated*.
3. **Nested "lines of defence."** The impact of a shock depends on successive buffers:
   - agro-ecosystem resilience;
   - farm-level management options;
   - community and household assets (the livelihood capitals: natural, physical, financial, human and social);
   - institutional safety nets.
   When one line is overwhelmed, the next is tested, and impact grows as defences crumble. The 2011 version uses three dimensions: agro-ecosystem resilience, socio-economic affluence and institutional capacity. It can be drawn as a 3-D space, with a trajectory toward the "vulnerable corner" over time.
4. **History is evidence.** Past collapses and crises (the Irish famine, the El Niño famines of the late 1800s, the Dust Bowl, Norse Greenland, the Maya) are natural experiments for the future (climate change, price shocks). Use them to extract transferable characteristics, then ask whether we are moving in the same direction. Celebrate long-run success (10,000 years of agriculture and repeated agricultural revolutions) while refusing complacency.
5. **Frameworks should be "simple but not simplistic."** A good framework:
   - is clear about the indicators it needs;
   - allows regions to be compared;
   - reveals trends through time;
   - reduces complexity without erasing local context.
   Name the macro–micro tension explicitly. Top-down models are communicable but can be "human-less"; bottom-up, participatory work is rich but slow and hard to compare. Resolve the tension by pairing a simple heuristic with locally grounded indicators.
6. **Integrate frameworks rather than pick one.** Introduce framework A, apply it, state its blind spot, introduce framework B to fill it, then synthesize into a combined procedure ("first assess…, then assess…"). Close by naming what the combined approach still misses (culture, institutions, power).
7. **Competing narratives produce competing solutions.** Use the grid–group Cultural Theory of Risk lens. Food-security debates divide into:
   - technocratic/individualist (productivity);
   - managerial/bureaucratic (rational management);
   - social welfare (equity);
   - communitarian/ecological (holism).
   Treat each as laudable but incomplete. Argue for a **portfolio** of policies, with explicit acknowledgement that tradeoffs are inevitable, and de-politicize the debate.
8. **Reconciliation, not either/or.** Production must be reconciled with planetary health ("Agriculture 5.0"). Technology is necessary but "not a panacea" without attention to gender, markets, low-cost tools and justice. Reject binaries such as global vs local, organic vs conventional, or livestock as all-bad. Show the conditions under which each holds.
9. **Structures and incentives shape behaviour.** Farmers' practices follow tenure, prices, contracts and programs, so test the assumptions policy rests on. Fraser 2004 found that long leases did not replicate the conservation effect of ownership. Look for intervening variables (for example, payments for set-asides) that override expected effects. In retail and consumption work, look for "fits" and "misfits" between actors' strategies: supermarkets were found to be simultaneously enabling and limiting dietary transition.
10. **Scale follows pathways, not jurisdictions.** Ecological boundaries rarely match political ones. Pick the analytical scale from the pathway that carries the problem (watersheds for floods and nutrients, airsheds, field connectivity for pests, transport corridors for disease). Collect data at the finest resolution possible and make aggregation transparent.
11. **Mismatches reveal system failure.** A core analytical move is to compare what a system *produces* with what is *needed* (KC et al. 2018: global agriculture over-produces grains, fats and sugars and under-produces fruit, vegetables and protein), then lay out pathways that close the gap with land and greenhouse-gas consequences quantified.
12. **Models are learning devices, not oracles.** Mediated or system-dynamics modelling runs as a sequence: narrative, conceptual model (causal loops, stocks and flows), qualitative vulnerability analysis, quantitative simulation. Simulations generate hypotheses about leverage points; they are not predictions.

## 3. Paper architectures

Choose the one that matches the paper type and adapt it to the journal.

**A. Conceptual or historical synthesis** (Fraser 2003; 2006 *Ecol. Complexity*; 2013 *GEC*)
1. *Hook.* Open with a vivid, dated, quantified event (who, when, how many), then pivot: aside from its significance, the case is a stark example of a broader problem.
2. *The debate.* Scholars have struggled to link X and Y; simplistic or deterministic accounts have failed, leaving an intellectual gap.
3. *Purpose.* "The purpose of this paper is to…", often as numbered objectives (1–3).
4. *Framework A.* Define it in plain language, apply it to the case with concrete evidence, and end with the insight it gives.
5. *Bridge.* "This assessment, however, does not provide insight into…"
6. *Framework B.* Define, apply and draw the insight, as for A.
7. *Synthesis.* Show what each framework misses and how they couple, as a stepwise procedure. Include a heuristic figure if possible.
8. *Remaining gap.* Name what the combined approach still cannot capture.
9. *Conclusion.* State the lesson for the present or future, the next steps, and a modest claim ("this paper has made a modest attempt…").

**B. Empirical case testing an assumption** (Fraser 2004; Capmourteres et al. 2018; Gravely & Fraser 2018)
1. State the widely held assumption and the literature behind it.
2. Explain why it matters: policy has been built on it.
3. Ask the pointed question: "However, does X actually deliver Y?"
4. State the purpose and testable hypotheses.
5. Justify the locale with "three reasons" that turn it into a natural experiment by controlling confounders.
6. Give methods with the logic of the proxy explained (for example, crop choice as an indicator of a conservation horizon).
7. Report results.
8. In the discussion, number the conclusions ("Four primary conclusions…"), including the *unexpected* result and the intervening variable that explains it.
9. Conclude with policy implications and the methodological contribution (for example, cheap proxy data can replace decades of monitoring).

**C. Comparative cases or a framework editorial** (Fraser et al. 2006 *JEM*; Fraser et al. 2011 *E&S*)
1. Name the tension (top-down vs bottom-up; generality vs context).
2. Set out three purposefully different cases.
3. Give a parallel description of each.
4. Draw cross-case lessons as numbered themes.
5. Set out the operational issues that remain (scale, aggregation, linking to decision forums).
6. Conclude with a short list of design principles.

**D. Commentary or perspective** (Fraser & Campbell 2019 *One Earth*)
1. Start with a long historical arc (agricultural revolutions) and celebrate the achievement.
2. Turn: "Even still, problems exist", followed by quantified planetary costs.
3. Set out the reconciliation imperative and coin or name the agenda.
4. Give numbered features of the needed transition, each with a "but…" complexity.
5. Offer a historical exemplar (a person or episode) as a moral model.
6. Close with a forward-looking call for interdisciplinary, applied work.

## 4. Paragraph- and section-level moves

Paraphrase these templates; do not reuse Fraser's sentences.

- **Concede, then complicate.** "On the surface, X is impressive… However, on closer inspection…"
- **Name the question explicitly.** Fraser uses genuine questions mid-argument ("What made the system so vulnerable that a common problem…?"; "Are we headed in the same direction?"). Use one or two per paper, at turning points.
- **Signpost lists.** "First… Second… Third… Finally…"; "Three main points can be drawn…"; "From this analysis two broad conclusions stand out."
- **End each paragraph with a synthesis sentence.** "Therefore, the situation can be characterized as…"; "Looked at in this way, …"
- **End each section with a bridge** that names what the section could not explain, so that the next section feels necessary.
- **Use a challenge-by-analogy.** "Just as [field] identified the characteristics of vulnerable [systems] by studying past collapses, can we identify…?"
- **Give a disaggregated explanation.** Replace a single-cause story (Malthusian collapse; government policy alone) with a breakdown of channels (for example, loss of indirect, then direct, then transfer entitlements).
- **Let primary voices in.** Brief, attributed quotations from farmers at public hearings, historical reports, policy texts, media or cultural works: a single line that makes the stakes human. Keep them short.
- **Handle contrary scholarship.** Name the minority or revisionist reading (Fraser's Cunfer/Gregory footnote is the model), credit it, say why the argument still stands for *this* purpose, and move on.
- **Close with the decision-maker.** "This should be of considerable interest to policy makers because…", followed by the specific mechanism.

## 5. Sentence-level style

- **Register.** Plain, accessible academic prose for an interdisciplinary reader. Define concepts in one clause on first use ("Sen called these channels 'entitlements'"). Avoid stacked nominalizations.
- **Rhythm.** Mix short declarative sentences ("This has left an intellectual void.") with longer explanatory ones. Use the occasional fragment-free punchy line at a turn.
- **Discourse markers** (most frequent in the corpus): *However*, *For example*, *For instance*, *Therefore*, *Finally*, *Hence*, *As such*, *Overall*, *In light of*, *Specifically*. Avoid *Moreover/Furthermore* chains.
- **Hedging.** Calibrated, frequent and honest: *may*, *suggests*, *seems*, *it is likely*, *one can only imagine*. Strong verbs (*shows*, *demonstrates*) are reserved for direct evidence.
- **Pronouns.** Use "I" in single-authored work and "we" otherwise. Use the inclusive "we" for humanity or society ("as we face a future of climate uncertainty…").
- **Numbers.** Anchor claims in concrete quantities with a source and baseline (people per unit of arable land; percentage of emissions; yields relative to normal; hectares; years).
- **Figurative language.** Sparing (at most about one image per section) and drawn from systems and defence imagery: "accident waiting to happen", "lines of defence", "the fabric of rural life unravelled", "not a panacea", "wicked problem".
- **Tone.** Balanced and non-partisan. Simple answers are "at best misguided". Acknowledge legitimate goals on every side. Avoid hype, single-solution advocacy, moralizing and unhedged forecasts.
- **Spelling.** Canadian (behaviour, centre, defence, program, analyze/characterize), unless the target journal's style differs. Write "tradeoffs" as one word, as Fraser does, unless the journal hyphenates.

## 6. Evidence and citation rules

- **Never invent a citation, quotation, statistic or DOI.** Where support is needed and not supplied, write `[CITE: claim — needs source]`.
- Give DOIs for every reference supplied. Flag any reference whose details are uncertain.
- Mark the epistemic status of key claims in drafts and notes, in line with the user's preference:
  - **established** (strong consensus or direct evidence);
  - **contested** (live debate, with both sides cited);
  - **inference** (the author's argument).
  In prose, this maps onto hedging strength.
- Prefer primary data and original studies over syntheses. State the data year and scale.
- When a framework from the corpus is used (Sen, panarchy, livelihood capitals, grid–group, mediated modelling), cite the original source the user holds. Do not cite Fraser for ideas he borrowed.

## 7. Applying the lens to Dr. Bodrud-Doza's domains

These are *applications* of Fraser's moves to the user's work, not claims Fraser made. Present them as the author's own reasoning.

- **Precision conservation and BMPs in tile-drained Great Lakes watersheds.**
  - Tenure and incentive logic (rented vs owned fields, lease length, cost-share and set-aside payments as intervening variables).
  - Profit and yield-stability maps as a bridge between production and conservation (Capmourteres et al. 2018).
  - Scale following pathways: the subwatershed or tile-drainage network rather than the municipality. Fine-resolution data with transparent aggregation to decision units.
- **Food-system stress-testing and anticipatory action.**
  - The small-trigger, large-impact logic.
  - Lines of defence as the stress-test architecture: which buffer fails first, and what happens next.
  - Historical analogues as calibration cases.
  - Early vs delayed response framed as defences that hold or crumble.
  - The portfolio and tradeoff logic for response options.
  - Mediated modelling (narrative, conceptual model, simulation) as a co-design method with decision-makers.
- **Earth observation and GeoAI.**
  - Technology is necessary but "not a panacea".
  - The digital divide and access.
  - Indicators that are simple but not simplistic, comparable across regions and able to reveal trends through time.

## 8. Drafting workflow

1. Confirm the mode and the six setup items (Section 1).
2. Write the one-sentence central claim and the "shock vs context" question the paper answers.
3. Choose the architecture (A–D) and outline it, with one line per paragraph stating its purpose and evidence.
4. Choose the frameworks. For each, decide what it explains, what it misses and how it couples with the others.
5. Draft section by section:
   - open with the hook;
   - end each paragraph with a synthesis sentence;
   - end each section with a bridge;
   - put a numbered list in the purpose statement and in the conclusions.
6. Insert `[CITE: …]` wherever support is not yet in hand. Never fill one from memory.
7. Self-check against `evans-reviewing` (the scorecard dimensions), then fix all Major issues before delivery.
8. Deliver the draft with a short note listing the assumptions made, open `[CITE]` slots and claims tagged *contested* or *inference*.

## 10. Limits of this style model

- The corpus covers 2003–2019. It does not include Fraser's more recent work on food-system resilience, anticipatory action or stress-testing. Ask the user to add those papers if the draft depends on his current framing.
- Seven papers are Fraser-led. In the three co-authored papers the prose reflects the first authors.
- Style is a means, not a constraint. Where a target journal's genre (for example, a hydrological modelling paper) does not suit a historical hook or a narrative frame, keep the transferable moves (shock vs context, lines of defence, tradeoffs, scale follows pathways, honest limits) and drop the genre-specific ones.
